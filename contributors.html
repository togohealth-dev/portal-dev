Method & honesty notes

Hours are estimated per project, not per line. A project that touches only the frontend counts 2.5h; one that touches the Rust backend or a migration counts 11h. That is the whole formula — there is no lines-of-code term.

Why line count was dropped. Earlier versions charged a flat 55 lines/hr. Measured against what people actually remember spending, that model was badly wrong in both directions: Riley wrote 531 effective lines in one project that took about 3h (177 lines/hr), while Morgan wrote 405 lines across four projects that took about 10h (40 lines/hr). Same file types, same stack, a 4× difference in rate — so lines were measuring typing, not effort. What actually predicts the number is how many separate projects you carried and whether each one reached the backend: understanding the problem, wiring it in, running it on localhost, and the PR round trip cost roughly the same whether the diff is 14 lines or 800.

Calibration and its limits. The two rates are fitted to three anecdotal totals from Greg — Greg ~50h, Morgan ~10h, Riley ~3h — and reproduce them to within 0.6h in aggregate (50.5 / 10.0 / 2.5). That is three data points against two parameters, so the fit is close to saturated: treat the shape (per-project cost, backend multiplier ~4×) as the finding, and the exact 2.5 and 11 as provisional. Every completed project that gets its real hours recorded tightens them.

Attribution is by PR author. Commit authorship was unusable — 77 of 110 commits are recorded as invalid-email-address, mapping to no GitHub account. /pull-pr-code-only rewrites authorship to whoever ran the import, so a synced PR credits the importer; #20 (MFA) was corrected to Scott on that basis and is the one known case. The other synced PRs are treated as their author’s own upstream work.

Scott is excluded from this summary. He reviews and gatekeeps rather than being counted as a build contributor here, so the projects whose PRs he opened do not appear.
